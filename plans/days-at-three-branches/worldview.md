# Worldview: Crane Reach

## The world in brief

Crane Reach is a remote district of reed flats, terraced hills, raised roads, and aging magic works. The Court still rules on paper, but it can no longer keep the peace. Local powers gather soldiers under their own banners, claim to act in the Court's name, and fight short wars over roads, harvests, towers, and working magic.

Three Branches is a small village in the Reach. It stands where a river divides into three channels and an old road crosses them. The village is useful enough to attract merchants, travelers, and rumors, but not important enough for any banner to hold securely. Ordinary life continues while skirmishes move through the surrounding countryside.

This is a hard but livable world. People repair tools, tend fields, trade, gossip, make plans, and joke about their troubles. War is common, but it is not the only thing in their lives.

## The Court, the banners, and the temples

The Court is the accepted center of the realm. Its seal still appears on coins, laws, appointments, and land claims. Its roads and measures are used everywhere. Few people expect its soldiers to arrive when trouble starts.

Local lords, towns, and military companies organize under banners. Temples keep their own guards and may support a banner or remain independent. Each power claims lawful authority, often using an old office, disputed inheritance, temple charter, or contradictory Court order as proof. A lord may protect a road one season and collect ruinous tolls on it the next. A temple may support a lord's claim, refuse him access to a warded crossing, or bargain with his rival.

The larger temples are powers in their own right. They hold fields, workshops, towers, and rights to important magic sites. Farmers and traders give them part of a harvest or profit in exchange for repairs, safe storage, mediation, and protection. Their leaders seek peace when it preserves their holdings, but they can fortify their compounds and field trained guards when bargaining fails.

Most wars remain small. Soldiers are scarce, fields must still be worked, and working infrastructure is more valuable intact than destroyed. Rival banners therefore raid, patrol, threaten, and skirmish. They fight for crossings, supply stores, and magic sites, then withdraw before a local victory becomes an expensive war.

## Magic and work

Magic is the ordinary word for a powerful force found in certain stones, waters, storms, and living things. Most people can use a forever lamp, talkative toy, or automated broom without learning anything about magic. They do cost a lot, though, and can be unreliable. A village craftsperson is more likely to replace a cracked magic core or trace a broken line than call for a wizard.

Only a rare few, commonly called wizards, can directly sense, shape, or command magic with great force. Wizards are valuable, feared, and usually attached to a temple, a lord's household, a military company, or a wealthy town. A skilled wizard can raise a ward, restore a failing beacon, quiet a dangerous magic site, or direct magic in battle. Their power is significant, but they are too rare for ordinary life to depend on their presence.

Temples train and shelter many of the known wizards, preserve old methods, and control access to some of the strongest magic works. A major temple may contain shrines, workshops, granaries, guest rooms, walls, and a guarded magic site. It can be a place of learning and refuge while also acting as a landlord, trading power, and military stronghold. Temples differ in their teachings and loyalties, and no single temple speaks for all of them.

Old magic works are stronger than most new ones, but their builders and original purposes are largely forgotten. Communities reuse whatever still functions. A beacon tower may become a watch post. A damaged ward may protect one side of a field and make the other side dangerous.

## Crane Reach

Crane Reach follows a broad river through wet lowlands and dry terraced uplands. White cranes, tall reeds, red pines, stone bells, lantern posts, and roofed roadside shrines give the district its visual identity. Broken stretches of raised road divide the landscape into natural routes, crossings, and choke points.

The Reach was once connected by a chain of magic beacons and warded towers. Enough of this network survives to make the district valuable. Not enough survives to make it stable. A working pump, bridge ward, or beacon can support several villages, so even minor sites can become the cause of a battle.

Places should be visually distinct and immediately understandable. A bell marks communication. A lantern marks safety or stored magic. A bridge, gate, well, or tower explains why people gather there and why banners might fight over it.

## Three Branches

Three Branches is the setting of the RPG game. Its stable features are:

- A central well with an old magic pump
- A small market beside the raised road
- An inn used by merchants, messengers, and passing soldiers
- A repair shed that maintains tools, lamps, and ward fittings
- An old beacon bell that works intermittently
- Homes and fields spread along the three river channels

The village has no fixed mood or story. It can feel prosperous, anxious, welcoming, secretive, neglected, or ready to flee, depending on the student's design goal. Its people may favor a banner, resent all banners, or avoid discussing politics. NPC roles, personalities, relationships, and rumors remain open for students to author.

The player is simply a visitor. The setting does not prescribe the visitor's origin, profession, loyalties, or purpose.

## The two games

The RPG game shows ordinary life in Three Branches. Its routines, conversations, memories, and rumors make the larger conflict visible without requiring a scripted plot. Villagers can discuss a closed road, a passing patrol, a temple demand, a failed ward, or a nearby beacon changing hands.

The tactical game shows clashes elsewhere in Crane Reach. Its armies are detachments serving rival banners, temples, or alliances between them. Battlefields can include roads, terraces, river crossings, beacon sites, and towers. Capture points represent useful ground or infrastructure, but the setting does not change their mechanics.

Local communication has the same character in both games. People use shouts, runners, bells, lantern signals, and simple magic beacons. In the village these carry warnings and rumors. In battle they carry target calls, movement signals, and requests for help.

## Naming guide

World concepts use familiar words.

Place names combine plain landscape, color, material, weather, plant, animal, or built-object words. Names such as Ash Steps, Stone Bell, Lantern Field, Red Ford, and Pine Gate fit the setting. Cranes, reeds, terraces, bells, lanterns, pines, and shrines provide an eastern character without requiring words borrowed from another language.

Future lore should remain minimal. The setting does not need a detailed cosmology, dynasty list, fixed temple doctrine, ancient timeline, fixed faction roster, or canonical plot unless a game feature directly requires one.
